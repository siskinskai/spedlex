==============================================================================

WHAT SPEDLEX IS

Parents, teachers and advocates increasingly use AI tools to understand an evaluation report, prepare for an IEP meeting, or draft a letter. But those documents are full of a child's private information. The safe way to use an AI tool is to remove the personal details (names, birth dates, school) from the document first, before anything is pasted in.

The software that does this has a hard time with special education, because it can't tell a child's name from the jargon. A careful tool blacks out "ARD committee" or "present levels" along with the names, and the AI can no longer make sense of the document. A careless tool lets a name slip through.

SPEDLex is the missing piece: an open, cited list of special-education terms (the federal ones, plus each state's own names for things) that these tools can consult. They remove the child's identity and leave the vocabulary alone. Every entry cites the regulation or state document it came from. The list contains no student information of any kind. It currently holds 1,019 terms across 32 jurisdictions (states and D.C.), and Nevada is one of them.

If the list is right, a family can use an AI tool to understand their child's paperwork without exposing their child. That is the whole point.

==============================================================================

WHY WE NEED YOUR EXPERTISE

The Nevada entries were drafted by an AI model (the project says so openly) reading Nevada's published documents: NDE Special Education Rights of Parents and Students (June 2023); State of Nevada IEP form (July 2018); NDE special education policies page; rule sections cited as "NAC ...". Automated tests check the data's structure and safety rules. But no independent expert in Nevada special education has confirmed it yet. Across the whole project, 0 of 1,019 entries have been confirmed by an expert.

Only a person in the field can tell us:
  - whether a term is really used in Nevada the way a document suggests,
  - whether a form or a name is still current (some documents we used are years old),
  - whether two different names mean the same thing in practice, and
  - what is missing, because the paperwork families actually see is not always what the published rules describe.

A wrong entry hurts in both directions: a tool may expose something it should have hidden, or black out something a family needed to read.

Of the 87 Nevada entries, 54 are already checked against the text of a regulation, so we aren't asking whether those terms exist (Part B asks only about Nevada's names for some of them). 33 are "candidates": read from a form or notice, never confirmed by a person. Those are what we need you for.
