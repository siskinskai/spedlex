WHAT SPEDLEX IS

SPEDLex is a free, open list of U.S. special-education terms. Each entry is one concept (for example, "IEP Team") and records every name that official documents use for it: the federal term, each state's own name (Texas says "ARD committee," New York says "CSE," Connecticut says "PPT"), abbreviations and acronyms, and older names that still turn up in paperwork. Every entry cites the regulation or state document it came from. It currently holds 1,670 concepts across 42 jurisdictions (states and D.C.), and Virginia is one of them. It contains no student information of any kind.

What it is not: a dictionary or a legal guide. It records how documents name things, not what the law requires. It is not complete, and it does not claim that a name is the only one or the current one. That is part of what we are asking you about.

Software that works with special-education documents needs to recognize this vocabulary. SPEDLex serves two uses:

  1. Protecting a child's privacy. Parents, teachers and advocates increasingly use AI tools to understand an evaluation report, prepare for an IEP meeting, or draft a letter. Those documents are full of a child's private information, so the safe way is to remove the personal details (names, birth dates, school) before anything is pasted in. The software that does this can't tell a child's name from the jargon. A careful tool blacks out "ARD committee" or "present levels" along with the names, and the AI can no longer make sense of the document. A careless tool lets a name slip through. SPEDLex lets these tools remove the child's identity and leave the vocabulary alone.

  2. Getting the wording right. AI assistants, summarizers, search tools, and translation and plain-language tools often use another state's term, an outdated one, or the wrong meaning of an acronym (CDE is a different agency in California and in Colorado). With SPEDLex, a tool can answer a Virginia family in Virginia's own words, spell out acronyms correctly, flag older names, and keep terms intact when it summarizes, searches or translates.

If the list is right, a family can use an AI tool to understand their child's paperwork without exposing their child, and have it explained in their own state's words. That is the whole point.

The Virginia entries were drafted by an AI model (the project says so openly) reading Virginia's published documents: VDOE Virginia Family's Guide to Special Education (May 2023); VDOE Virginia Procedural Safeguards Notice (rev. Sept. 2024); 8VAC20-81-10; VDOE Virginia Procedural Safeguards Notice (Town Hall copy, rev. Sept. 2013); Fairfax County Public Schools; VDOE Special Education for Families page; VDOE Virginia Family's Guide to Special Education (May 2023), pp. 19-20; VDOE Virginia Procedural Safeguards Notice (rev. Sept. 2024), cover; rule sections cited as "VDOE SPLAVC fact sheet (Aug. ...", "VDOE VAAP FAQ for Parents ...". Automated tests check the data's structure and safety rules. But no independent expert in Virginia special education has confirmed it yet. Across the whole project, 0 of 1,670 entries have been confirmed by an expert.

Only a person in the field can tell us:
  - whether a term is really used in Virginia the way a document suggests,
  - whether a form or a name is still current (some documents we used are years old),
  - whether two different names mean the same thing in practice (this is how a tool decides to say "IEP Team" or "ARD committee" to a family), and
  - what is missing, because the paperwork families actually see is not always what the published rules describe.

A wrong entry causes harm in three ways: a privacy tool may expose something it should have hidden, it may black out something a family needed to read, or an AI assistant may give a family another state's word or an outdated one.

We are not asking you to define terms or interpret the law. We are asking whether a name is real, current and used the way we say.

Of the 176 Virginia entries, 106 are already checked against the text of a regulation, so we aren't asking whether those terms exist (Part B asks only about Virginia's names for some of them). 70 are "candidates": read from a form or notice, never confirmed by a person. Those are what we need you for.
