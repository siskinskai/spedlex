@@ what
WHAT SPEDLEX IS

Parents, teachers and advocates increasingly use AI tools to understand an evaluation report, prepare for an IEP meeting, or draft a letter. But those documents are full of a child's private information. The safe way to use an AI tool is to remove the personal details (names, birth dates, school) from the document first, before anything is pasted in.

The software that does this has a hard time with special education, because it can't tell a child's name from the jargon. A careful tool blacks out "ARD committee" or "present levels" along with the names, and the AI can no longer make sense of the document. A careless tool lets a name slip through.

SPEDLex is the missing piece: an open, cited list of special-education terms (the federal ones, plus each state's own names for things) that these tools can consult. They remove the child's identity and leave the vocabulary alone. Every entry cites the regulation or state document it came from. The list contains no student information of any kind. It currently holds {{N_CONCEPTS}} terms across {{N_STATES}} jurisdictions (states and D.C.), and {{STATE}} is one of them.

If the list is right, a family can use an AI tool to understand their child's paperwork without exposing their child. That is the whole point.

@@ why
WHY WE NEED YOUR EXPERTISE

The {{STATE}} entries were drafted by an AI model (the project says so openly) reading {{STATE}}'s published documents: {{DOC_LIST}}. Automated tests check the data's structure and safety rules. But no independent expert in {{STATE}} special education has confirmed it yet. Across the whole project, {{N_EXPERT}} of {{N_CONCEPTS}} entries have been confirmed by an expert.

Only a person in the field can tell us:
  - whether a term is really used in {{STATE}} the way a document suggests,
  - whether a form or a name is still current (some documents we used are years old),
  - whether two different names mean the same thing in practice, and
  - what is missing, because the paperwork families actually see is not always what the published rules describe.

A wrong entry hurts in both directions: a tool may expose something it should have hidden, or black out something a family needed to read.

Of the {{N_STATE}} {{STATE}} entries, {{N_VALIDATED}} are already checked against the text of a regulation, so we aren't asking whether those terms exist (Part B asks only about {{STATE}}'s names for some of them). {{N_CAND}} are "candidates": read from a form or notice, never confirmed by a person. Those are what we need you for.

@@ how
HOW TO ANSWER

  1. Hit Reply and keep this message in your reply. Just type in the
     brackets.
  2. Put Y (yes), N (no) or ? (not sure) inside the brackets, like [Y].
  3. To add a comment or a correction, start a new line under the item with
     "Comment:". Corrections are the most valuable thing you can send.
  4. Skip anything you like. Partial answers are welcome. If you have only
     a few minutes, do Part A and stop.
  5. Please don't include anything about a real student: no names, schools,
     birth dates or text from a real IEP. Tell us about terms, not children.

You don't need to open any document. Answer from your own experience. The documents we used are listed at the end if you want to check one.

WHAT HAPPENS TO YOUR ANSWERS: a maintainer enters them in the project's public review log. Terms you confirm are marked "expert-accepted". Terms you correct are fixed or removed. Crediting you is entirely your choice (see "Credit" below). If you opt in, your name and role are written into the review log, which is part of the open dataset and stays public. We'll also add you to the project's CONTRIBUTORS list on GitHub. If you choose, your affiliation appears there too, but never in the review log. If you'd rather not be named, we record you as anonymous. The data is open (CC BY 4.0).
