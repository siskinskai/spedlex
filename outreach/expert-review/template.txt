@@ what
WHAT SPEDLEX IS

SPEDLex is a free, open list of U.S. special-education terms. Each entry is one concept (for example, "IEP Team") and records every name that official documents use for it: the federal term, each state's own name (Texas says "ARD committee," New York says "CSE," Connecticut says "PPT"), abbreviations and acronyms, and older names that still turn up in paperwork. Every entry cites the regulation or state document it came from. It currently holds {{N_CONCEPTS}} concepts across {{N_STATES}} jurisdictions (states and D.C.), and {{STATE}} is one of them. It contains no student information of any kind.

What it is not: a dictionary or a legal guide. It records how documents name things, not what the law requires. It is not complete, and it does not claim that a name is the only one or the current one. That is part of what we are asking you about.

Software that works with special-education documents needs to recognize this vocabulary. SPEDLex serves two uses:

  1. Protecting a child's privacy. Parents, teachers and advocates increasingly use AI tools to understand an evaluation report, prepare for an IEP meeting, or draft a letter. Those documents are full of a child's private information, so the safe way is to remove the personal details (names, birth dates, school) before anything is pasted in. The software that does this can't tell a child's name from the jargon. A careful tool blacks out "ARD committee" or "present levels" along with the names, and the AI can no longer make sense of the document. A careless tool lets a name slip through. SPEDLex lets these tools remove the child's identity and leave the vocabulary alone.

  2. Getting the wording right. AI assistants, summarizers, search tools, and translation and plain-language tools often use another state's term, an outdated one, or the wrong meaning of an acronym (CDE is a different agency in California and in Colorado). With SPEDLex, a tool can answer a {{STATE}} family in {{STATE}}'s own words, spell out acronyms correctly, flag older names, and keep terms intact when it summarizes, searches or translates.

If the list is right, a family can use an AI tool to understand their child's paperwork without exposing their child, and have it explained in their own state's words. That is the whole point.

@@ why
WHY WE NEED YOUR EXPERTISE

The {{STATE}} entries were drafted by an AI model (the project says so openly) reading {{STATE}}'s published documents: {{DOC_LIST}}. Automated tests check the data's structure and safety rules. But no independent expert in {{STATE}} special education has confirmed it yet. Across the whole project, {{N_EXPERT}} of {{N_CONCEPTS}} entries have been confirmed by an expert.

Only a person in the field can tell us:
  - whether a term is really used in {{STATE}} the way a document suggests,
  - whether a form or a name is still current (some documents we used are years old),
  - whether two different names mean the same thing in practice (this is how a tool decides to say "IEP Team" or "ARD committee" to a family), and
  - what is missing, because the paperwork families actually see is not always what the published rules describe.

A wrong entry causes harm in three ways: a privacy tool may expose something it should have hidden, it may black out something a family needed to read, or an AI assistant may give a family another state's word or an outdated one.

We are not asking you to define terms or interpret the law. We are asking whether a name is real, current and used the way we say.

Of the {{N_STATE}} {{STATE}} entries, {{N_VALIDATED}} are already checked against the text of a regulation, so we aren't asking whether those terms exist (Part B asks only about {{STATE}}'s names for some of them). {{N_CAND}} are "candidates": read from a form or notice, never confirmed by a person. Those are what we need you for.

@@ how
HOW TO ANSWER

  1. Hit Reply and keep this message in your reply. Just type in the
     brackets.
  2. Put Y (yes), N (no) or ? (not sure) inside the brackets, like [Y].
  3. To add a comment or a correction, start a new line under the item with
     "Comment:". Corrections are the most valuable thing you can send.
  4. Skip anything you like. Partial answers are welcome. If you have only
     a few minutes, do Part A and stop.
  5. Please don't include anything about a real student: no names, schools,
     birth dates or text from a real IEP. Tell us about terms, not children.

You don't need to open any document. Answer from your own experience. The documents we used are listed at the end if you want to check one.

WHAT HAPPENS TO YOUR ANSWERS: a maintainer enters them in the project's public review log. Terms you confirm are marked "expert-accepted". Terms you correct are fixed or removed. Crediting you is entirely your choice (see "Credit" below). If you opt in, your name and role are written into the review log, which is part of the open dataset and stays public. We'll also add you to the project's CONTRIBUTORS list on GitHub. If you choose, your affiliation appears there too, but never in the review log. If you'd rather not be named, we record you as anonymous. The data is open (CC BY 4.0).
