A safelist for special-education vocabulary

Caregivers increasingly use AI tools to understand IEPs and evaluations. Doing that safely means removing the child's personal information first, with a de-identification tool. Those tools need to know which words are special-education terms that must survive, and no standard vocabulary told them. SPEDLex is that vocabulary. The core comes from the IDEA Part B regulations. Each state layer is drawn from that state's procedural safeguards notice, its family or IEP guide, and its special education regulations. Every concept carries its surface forms, every source that attests it, and a privacy-risk grade that tells a scrubber when it may protect the term. Purple rows are placeholders for later harvests.

The unit of the list

Anatomy of one concept record

Concept, not word

One ID holds the regulatory phrase, the acronym, and the older forms that still appear in IEPs. An IEP that says "PLOP" and one that says "present levels" resolve to the same thing.

Case rule per string

Phrases match case-insensitively. Acronyms match case-sensitively, so "ED" is protected while the name "Ed" is not.

Citation on every record

Each record points to a CFR paragraph. A reviewer can open the source in one click, and the count of terms with no MeSH or SNOMED mapping becomes the measured gap.

Risk grade decides protection

phrase_or_token terms are safe anywhere. never_bare terms like "Tourette" or "Kaufman" are protected only inside their full phrase, so a surname is never sheltered.

What the states added

Each state was first read from three documents: its procedural safeguards notice, its main IEP document, and its eligibility rule. Texas attested 96 concepts and 60 were new. Nevada attested 87 and 36 were new. Virginia attested 108 and 49 were new. That is more than Nevada because Virginia's regulation defines many terms of its own. Even so, more than half of what Virginia uses was already in the list. Most of its new records are diplomas, credits, and Virginia agencies. California attested 95 and only 35 were new. Of the other 60, 51 came straight from the federal core, because California's rules track federal wording closely. Its distinct vocabulary sits in services and structures (DIS, RSP, SELPA, nonpublic schools). New York attested 117 and 45 were new. It reused 58 federal concepts and 8 testing accommodations first sourced from Texas. Its new vocabulary is committees, class ratios, Regents credentials, and preschool services.

Florida attested 75 concepts (23 new), Pennsylvania 57 (28 new), and Illinois 79 (28 new). By the eighth state, half to two-thirds of the concepts a state uses are already in the list. What stays new is each state's agencies, forms, assessments, credentials, and its own way of naming placements.

The next seven states added far less. Massachusetts attested 61 concepts (22 new), Minnesota 50 (13), Missouri 42 (14), Louisiana 42 (10), Kansas 46 (12), Utah 73 (14), and Washington 40 (7). A second Pennsylvania pass added 8 records for preschool and gifted terms. The new records are almost all state agencies, assessment names, and each state's own disability labels.

Tennessee attested 36 concepts (7 new), Idaho 44 (11), Oregon 60 (13), and Georgia 44 (10). Across nineteen states, each new state now adds about ten concepts.

A second Texas pass read TEA's 2025–26 STAAR Alternate 2 and Accommodations educator guides and the 2024 Dyslexia Handbook. It attested 112 concepts and 84 were new, almost all reading and writing skills and testing supports. Procedural vocabulary is converging across states. Skill and testing vocabulary is a separate, larger pool that the regulations never touch.

Class ratios are numbers

New York names special classes by ratio: "12:1+1" is twelve students, one teacher, one aide. They are protected only as exact strings with word boundaries, so "12:1" never matches inside a time like 12:15, and none trip the scrubber's phone, ZIP, or dose patterns.

Name dictionaries contain state names

Common first-name lists include "Virginia", "Florida", "Georgia", and "Carolina". A scrubber that uses one will turn "Virginia Alternate Assessment Program" into "[NAME] Alternate Assessment Program". Either protect the full phrases first, as SPEDLex does, or remove state names from the dictionary and rely on a household-names list to catch a student who really is named Georgia. "Rosa" (Rosa's Law) is the same problem with no easy fix, so the phrase stays protected.

Dyslexia vocabulary now has sources

The handbook's evaluation domains and instruction components gave cited records for phonological awareness, rapid automatized naming, orthographic processing, decoding, prosody, syllabication, and morphology, plus dysgraphia and its handwriting, graphomotor, and transcription areas. Phonological awareness and extended time were placeholders until now. "Standard protocol dyslexia instruction" is kept as a legacy string, since HB 3928 dropped the distinction but older IEPs still use it.

Legacy terms are current text

Nevada's rule headings still read "mental retardation" and "serious emotional disturbance", amended as recently as 2019. A safelist that dropped outdated labels would strip the state's own citations from an IEP.

Names, places, and acronyms

Texas added REED (Reed), ADA (Ada), TEA (tea), IDA (Ida), ALTA (Alta), and TIDE (tide), all protected only as all-caps matches. Acronyms also collide across states: CSA is Virginia's Children's Services Act and the California Spanish Assessment, NPS is a California nonpublic school as well as a common abbreviation for the safeguards notice, and DESE names both the Massachusetts and Missouri education departments. "Texas", "Nevada", and "Virginia" are all given names in the SSA list, and "SOL" is too, so a place-name list belongs in review. Nevada's form codes services as A, C, or D, and single letters are never safelisted. "SRO" means state review officer in Nevada hearings but school resource officer in discipline records.

State records sourced from PDFs have review status "candidate": page numbers came from a text extraction. Records from the Texas and Nevada administrative codes were checked against the rule text. Nevada's IEP form is dated 2018, so its state-assessment names may be outdated.

Where the remaining terms come from

Coverage by category

Counts are concepts, not strings. The regulations give a dense, citable core. Skill constructs, instruments, and score language barely appear in them and come from the corpus and instrument harvests.

Integrating with a scrubber

Where the safelist runs

The safelist has to run before any redaction tier. Otherwise a name dictionary can redact "Johnson" out of "Woodcock-Johnson IV" before the safelist ever sees the phrase. Protected spans are masked, the scrubber runs unchanged, then the spans are restored. The Python package's Safelist.protect() does the masking and restoring. The steps below show a typical layered scrubber.
